# Studying long-text subjects with ADHD: what the evidence supports

Research note, 2026-11. Question: which of a Gemini-generated list of study ideas should the kiosk app use to help a 10-year-old with ADHD (Ε΄ Δημοτικού) learn the prose-heavy subjects: Ιστορία, Γεωγραφία, Θρησκευτικά, Φυσικά? Sources were read as full text where noted, otherwise as abstracts (marked *abstract*). No number below is quoted from memory.

## Summary

1. **Solid: quiz after reading, with the right answer shown after every try.** Retrieval practice is the best-supported study technique (Dunlosky 2013: "high utility"). It works for 10-year-olds (Karpicke 2016) and in Grade 7 classrooms (McDermott 2014). Feedback roughly doubles the effect (Rowland 2014: g 0.73 with feedback vs 0.39 without).
2. **The ADHD caveat on quizzes.** College students with ADHD gain as much as their peers when they get most first attempts right (Knouse 2016: d 0.50 vs 0.57). They gain nothing when free recall of prose is too hard (Dudukovic 2015). Easy, cued questions with feedback come first. No study tested 10-year-olds with ADHD.
3. **Solid: come back to it on another day.** Spacing is the other high-utility technique, and it worked in Grade 5 classrooms. The app's retries (#136) already do this.
4. **Solid for behaviour, not for learning: frequent small rewards.** Behavioural classroom management and daily report cards are well-established ADHD treatments (Pelham & Fabiano 2008; Evans 2014/2018). Group studies show they move effort and conduct more than achievement (DuPaul 2012: academic g≈0.43, not significant). Stars per chunk keep her going. The questions do the teaching.
5. **Plausible: short chunks, a choice of order, a calm visible timer, movement allowed, a parent nearby, exercise beforehand.** Each rests on small or indirect evidence but carries little risk.
6. **Mixed: dropping multiple choice for typed answers.** Recall-type questions teach more in the lab (Rowland: initial cued recall g 0.61 vs recognition 0.29). In classrooms, multiple choice with feedback did as well as short answer (McDermott 2014). In the one ADHD comparison, multiple choice did better (Halamish & Stern 2019, conference abstract). Keep multiple choice with feedback, and add recall as an extra rung, not a replacement.
7. **Doubtful to harmful: lockouts that grow after each error, combos that reset on a miss, hearts/lives, a 60-second timed "boss fight".** No study tested any of them in children. Everything nearby points against them for ADHD: penalties pile up harder (Furukawa 2017), emotional reactivity is high (Graziano & Garcia 2016: d 0.95), and a forced wait is the thing delay-averse children avoid.
8. **Harmful or useless: fidget spinners and chewing gum.** Spinners worsened attention (Graziano 2020). Gum worsened vigilance (Tucha 2010).
9. **Generative strategies are mostly not ready for age 10 without scaffolding.** Teach-back, writing a question and drawing are well-supported in adults. Brod's 2021 review finds drawing and questioning "largely ineffective until secondary school". Her own highlighting does not help school pupils, while highlighting chosen for her does. "Find the main ideas" with feedback is plausible: missing the central ideas is exactly the ADHD reading deficit. Read-aloud is the one accommodation with ADHD-specific randomised evidence. Chunking, frequent feedback and short focus periods with breaks are what NICE, AAP and CADDRA endorse.
10. **Fit with the app.** The app's existing choices match the evidence: the forgiving ladder, hints after a wrong try, «Δείξε μου», the retries, stars that only add, and the owl's voice. The punitive mechanics conflict with them and should be left out.

## Details

### Retrieval practice (quizzing) and feedback

- **General.** Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated two of ten techniques "high utility", practice testing and distributed practice, because they work across ages, abilities, materials and tests. Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving were rated moderate. Summarisation, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text and rereading were rated low.
- **Effect sizes (Rowland 2014, read in full, 159 studies, mostly adult lab work).**
  - Testing vs restudy overall: g 0.50 [0.42, 0.58].
  - With feedback, g 0.73; without, 0.39.
  - Cued-recall practice gave g 0.61, recognition practice 0.29.
- **Classroom evidence.** Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2019/2021, systematic review of 50 classroom experiments, n = 5,374) found that 57% of effects were medium or large. The benefit held across levels, subjects, formats and feedback timing.
- **Children.** Karpicke, Blunt & Smith (2016) ran three experiments with 88 children of mean age 10. All showed "robust retrieval practice effects", and the effect did not depend on reading comprehension or processing speed. Caveat: these were word lists, not prose.
- **Middle and high school (McDermott et al. 2014).** Quizzes with correct-answer feedback in Grade 7 science and high-school history raised unit and end-of-semester exam scores. Quizzing beat restudy. "Multiple-choice quizzing is as effective as short-answer quizzing."
- **ADHD, all studies in college students.** No study tested children with ADHD.
  - Knouse et al. (2016, *Clin Psychol Sci*, *abstract via secondary page*): 25 with ADHD, 75 without. Both groups showed a moderate testing effect, d 0.50 and 0.57.
  - Minear et al. (2023, read): 36 with self-reported ADHD, 36 controls. Testing helped everyone with no group interaction. Unmedicated students encoded worse to begin with, so practice "does not compensate for poor encoding".
  - Dudukovic et al. (2015, *abstract*): with free recall of prose passages, students with ADHD showed no testing benefit. The authors put this "in part" down to low recall on the practice test.
  - Stern & Halamish (2023, *abstract*): free recall after the whole text beat recall after each section on a test two days later, for both groups. However, neither beat restudy, and the recall of students with ADHD was less well ordered. They recommend whole-text recall together with strategies that organise the material.
  - Halamish & Stern (2019, conference abstract, no numbers): 36 + 36 students. Retrieval beat rereading for both groups, and multiple-choice practice beat short-answer practice on a short-answer test a week later: "students with ADHD can benefit from retrieval practice when it is adjusted to their cognitive abilities."
- **What this means.** For ADHD, the active ingredient is retrieval she can mostly succeed at, followed by the answer. Hard free recall of a whole page is where the ADHD benefit disappeared. Wilschut et al. (2026, *npj Sci Learn*) note that testing-effect research has hardly looked at learners with learning disabilities, so the extrapolation to a 10-year-old with ADHD is ours, not the literature's.

### Errors, hints and feedback timing

- **Errors followed by the answer are not harmful.** Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009, adults) found that failed retrieval attempts enhanced later learning when the answer followed. Butler & Roediger (2008) found that immediate and delayed feedback both raised later recall and cut "lure" intrusions from multiple choice. Without feedback, reading more multiple-choice lures increased false answers later (Roediger & Marsh 2005).
- **The answer should not sit on screen as the next item's lure.** Metcalfe & Eich (2019) found that after an error, people also "hyper-corrected" to *false* feedback.
- **Teacher-guided error discussion.** Metcalfe et al. (2025, Grade 9 algebra) found that learning from errors beat explicit instruction when the teacher engaged pupils with their errors interactively.
- **No evidence that errorful learning harms children or ADHD.** I found none, given that feedback follows.

### Spacing and re-queueing

- **Spacing in general.** Cepeda et al. (2006, 839 assessments) found that the best gap grows with how long the material must be kept. Days, not minutes, suit a school test a week or more away.
- **Spacing in Grade 5 (Sobel, Cepeda & Kapler 2011, figures from a secondary review).** Review spaced a week apart gave 20.8% retention after 5 weeks, against 7.5% for same-day review.
- **Rephrasing (Pan & Rickard 2018, read).** Testing transfers on average (d 0.40), and best across test formats and to inference questions.
  - It does not transfer to "untested materials seen during initial study". Bias-adjusted, that category was negative (d −0.32).
  - So a rephrased retry is fine and probably better than the same wording, but every fact that matters needs its own question. Quizzing three facts of a page does not teach the other ten.

### Prequestions and "prompt first"

- **Prequestions before reading (St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024, meta-analysis, read).**
  - The effect on the prequestioned facts is moderate, g 0.54 (k 97). The effect on everything else in the text is "virtually nonexistent", g 0.04.
  - In grade-school children the specific effect was g 0.22 against 0.62 in adults (k 20, wide CIs).
  - It concerns questions *before* reading. Gemini's "question first, options after 5 s" is a different thing: recall before recognition.
- **Recall before recognition.** It is the stronger retrieval (Rowland: cued recall 0.61 vs recognition 0.29). But it lowers first-try success, which is the ADHD risk above. Make it optional, or make it a rung, rather than enforcing a wait.

### Rewards, response cost and penalties

- **Delay aversion.** Children with ADHD choose to cut total delay, even at the cost of reward (Sonuga-Barke et al. 1992). The dual-pathway model (Sonuga-Barke 2002/2003) adds a motivational route next to inhibition.
- **Choice-impulsivity (Patros et al. 2016, meta-analysis, 28 tasks).** g 0.47, smaller in samples that included girls.
- **How the reinforcement schedule matters.** Reinforcement improves task performance in ADHD and typical children alike, "somewhat more prominent in AD/HD" (Luman et al. 2005, review of 22 studies). Lab studies disagree on whether reward *frequency* matters:
  - Luman et al. (2009): boys with ADHD were unaffected by reward frequency or magnitude.
  - De Meyer et al. (2019): no specific ADHD deficit; thinning the reinforcement gradually works.
  - Hulsbosch et al. (2023, 2026): mixed results; "more negative emotional expressions … irrespective of reinforcement condition".
  - A rich start that is then thinned is the defensible schedule.
- **School programmes.**
  - Behavioural classroom management, parent training and peer interventions are well-established (Pelham & Fabiano 2008; Evans et al. 2014, 2018).
  - DuPaul, Eckert & Vilardo (2012), 60 studies: academic outcomes g 0.43 between-subjects and 0.42 within, not significant; behaviour 0.72 within-subjects. Contingency management gave the largest behaviour effects, and academic interventions such as computer-aided instruction the largest academic effects.
  - Fabiano et al. (2010), daily report card RCT, n = 63: better classroom functioning and productivity, no added gain on achievement tests.
  - Stars buy time on task. The content and the questions have to carry the learning.
- **Extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, 128 studies).** Tangible rewards for engagement, completion or performance lower free-choice intrinsic motivation, d −0.40, −0.36 and −0.28, and "tended to be more detrimental for children". Positive feedback raised it (+0.33). For school prose she does not yet enjoy, that cost is small. It argues for informational praise ("you found 3 of 3 causes") alongside stars.
- **Response cost (losing points).**
  - It can work: Rapport et al. (1982), n = 2; Carlson & Tamm (2000), 22 + 22 children. But Carlson & Tamm found it did so "at the expense of decreased self-rated motivation on the low-interest task".
  - Furukawa et al. (2017; 145 ADHD, 65 typical; replicated in Japan in 2019) found children with ADHD "more sensitive to the cumulative effects of punishment". Their bias away from the punished option grew with time on task, and they missed rewards because of it.
  - Pfiffner et al. (1985) found praise alone not enough. Strong individual rewards carried an all-positive system.
- **Emotion.** In Graziano & Garcia (2016, 77 studies, n = 32,044), emotional reactivity/negativity in ADHD gave d 0.95 and emotion regulation d 0.80. Frustration persists into recovery (Derella et al. 2024). Shaw et al. (2014) call emotion dysregulation "a major contributor to impairment".

### Time pressure, timers, lockouts, combos, hearts

- **Direct evidence.** None found for any of these in children with ADHD, or in children at all (searched PubMed and the web).
- **Event rate (Metin et al. 2012, meta-analysis of Go/No-Go).** ADHD responses slow disproportionately at slow rates, and impulsive errors grow at fast rates. Pace matters both ways; it says nothing about countdowns over schoolwork.
- **Calm timer (Hallez & Vallier 2025, n = 44 typical 7–9-year-olds, *abstract*).** A visible Time Timer lowered anticipatory anxiety, did not change maths scores, and reduced off-task behaviour, most in children at risk for ADHD. A quarter of the children checked it more than 7 times in 5 minutes.
- **Verdict, by inference from the penalty and emotion evidence above:**
  - An exponential lockout is a growing penalty plus a forced wait right after a mistake.
  - A combo reset is a response cost that lands only on misses.
  - Hearts end the round on errors.
  - A 60-second clock adds speed pressure to all three.
  - Each targets the two things ADHD children are shown to be more sensitive to. The intended benefit, stopping rapid guessing, is better served by making guessing pointless: no reward for a wrong try, the hint and then the answer after it. The app already does that.
- **Gamification in general.** Sailer & Homner (2020, meta-analysis) found cognitive outcomes g 0.49, stable across rigorous studies, and motivational 0.36 and behavioural 0.25, both less stable; there were no ADHD samples. In Hanus & Fox (2015, university), a leaderboard plus badges lowered intrinsic motivation over a term. Lumsden et al. (2016) found games "highly engaging" with "mixed effects … on task performance". Peñuelas-Calvo et al. (2020/2022, 22 ADHD video-game studies) found them well accepted, but follow-up was short.

### Choice

- Choice of assignment reduced problem behaviour in a second-grader with ADHD (Powell & Nelson 1997). It raised engagement in pupils with emotional/behavioural disorders, and choice beat a yoked no-choice condition (Dunlap et al. 1994).
- A single-case meta-analysis (Shogren et al. 2004; 13 studies, 30 participants) found clinically significant reductions in problem behaviour.
- Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008, 41 studies): choice raises intrinsic motivation and effort, most "when 2 to 4 successive choices were given", for children more than adults, and when no reward follows the choice.
- "Demand avoidance" as an ADHD construct rests on one small correlational study (Lack et al. 2026). Design for choice, not for that label.

### Movement, fidgets, gum, noise, exercise

- **Movement and working memory (Sarver et al. 2015, read).** In 29 boys with ADHD and 23 typical boys aged 8–12, "higher rates of activity level predicted significantly better, but not normalized" phonological working memory in ADHD, and somewhat lower working memory in typical boys. The authors advise against "overcorrecting gross motor activity during academic tasks".
- **Movement and cognitive control (Hartanto et al. 2016, read).** 26 ADHD and 18 typical children aged 10–17 did a flanker task. Movement intensity was higher in ADHD on correct trials only (d 0.63), and within ADHD it was higher on correct than on incorrect trials (d 0.37). Small and correlational.
- **Seating.** Therapy balls raised in-seat behaviour and legible word output for 3 Grade 4 pupils with ADHD (Schilling et al. 2003, single-case ABAB). A 2025 *Frontiers in Pediatrics* review of sensory interventions found no significant effect of alternative seating (*search summary only*).
- **Fidget spinners.** Graziano, Garcia & Landis (2020), 60 children with ADHD of mean age 4.86, A-B-A-B in class: spinners went with "poorer attention across both phases".
- **Gum.** Tucha et al. (2010), crossover with 32 ADHD and 32 typical children: gum chewing "has detrimental effects on vigilance"; sustained attention was unchanged.
- **Acute exercise (Song et al. 2023, PLoS One, read).** 24 studies. Acute physical activity improved inhibitory control in ADHD children, SMD −0.36 [−0.57, −0.16] (a negative value means improvement), but not working memory (−0.07) or flexibility (−0.07). Chronic activity improved all three.
- **White/pink noise.** Not on the list, but low-cost. Nigg et al. (2024, JAACAP, *abstract via search*) pooled 13 studies: g 0.249 benefit in ADHD and g −0.212 harm in non-ADHD, with poor control conditions. It would help her and hurt a sibling at the same kiosk.

### Body doubling and a parent nearby

- **No controlled trial in children.**
- **Adults.** Eagle et al. (2024, survey of 220 adults) describe how people use it. Born (2024) found no significant effect in ADHD participants. In Ara et al. (2025, arXiv preprint, 12 adults in VR), output was faster with a human or AI "double" (bricks/min 8.49 alone vs 10.82 and 11.06), with no gain in accuracy.
- **Parent involvement.** Structured parent support for homework is well-supported. Family-School Success (Power et al. 2012, RCT, n = 199, grades 2–6) improved homework performance, using daily report cards and behavioural homework routines. That is a parent with a plan, not just a parent in the room.

### Reading prose with ADHD: chunking, main ideas, highlighting

- **Where the deficit is.** Children with ADHD (mean age 9.8), matched to controls on word reading, recalled *central* information worst. Working memory explained the most variance (Miller et al. 2013, *abstract*). Berthiaume (2006) finds that interventions aimed at behaviour and attention "may not address deficits in story comprehension". A child who decodes well can still lose the thread of a page.
- **Reading-intervention meta-analysis (Chan et al. 2023, read in full; 18 studies, N = 564).** Effects were large: g 1.11 on standardized tests in the high-quality studies. Most interventions targeted decoding, and comprehension-targeted ones did worse. No study targeted organisation, engagement or study skills. There is little to borrow for a fluent reader.
- **Main-idea strategy training.** TWA taught with self-regulated strategy development (SRSD) has two studies, both *abstract only*:
  - Rogevich & Perin (2008): 63 adolescent boys with behaviour disorders, 31 of them also with ADHD; written summaries improved, η² 0.42, and generalised to social-studies text.
  - Johnson, Reid & Mason (2012): 3 high-school students with ADHD; recall of main ideas improved and held for 4 weeks.
- **Segmenting (Rey et al. 2019, 56 investigations).** "Small to medium effects for retention and transfer". Mostly adults and multimedia.
- **Highlighting (Ponce, Mayer & Méndez 2022, 85 effect sizes).**

  | Who highlighted | Group | Effect |
  |---|---|---|
  | The learner | College | 0.39 |
  | The learner | School pupils | 0.24, not significant |
  | The instructor | College | 0.41 |
  | The instructor | School pupils | 0.48 |

  Dunlosky (2013) says highlighting "failed to help … children". So marks chosen by the author help her, and her own free marks do not, unless a check gives her feedback.
- **Verdict on "2 main ideas in yellow, 3 surprising facts in pink".** The first half is a main-idea task. It is plausible when the right answer is authored and checked. "Surprising facts" has no evidence and invites marking at random.

### Generative strategies: teaching, questions, drawing

- **Age is the main limit (Brod 2021, review).** All six strategies reviewed work for university students. In children, "practice testing, predicting" already work in lower elementary school, while "drawing, questioning" are "largely ineffective until secondary school".
- **Teaching.**
  - Kobayashi (2019, 28 studies): preparing to teach g 0.35; preparing and then teaching 0.56. Interactive teaching beat one-way explaining (Kobayashi 2019, *Front Psychol*).
  - Fiorella & Mayer (2014): "preparing to teach coupled with actually teaching led to greatest long-term learning".
  - Roscoe & Chi (2007): tutors show a "knowledge-telling bias". They learn from reflective explaining and from the learner's questions. A parent who only listens adds little; a parent who asks "why?" adds the active part.
  - Chase et al. (2009): Grade 8 students who taught an agent learned more, most of all the lower achievers. 5th graders tried harder and owned their errors more easily, because the agent "protects students' egos". That is effort, not measured learning.
- **Writing questions.**
  - Rosenshine, Meister & Chapman (1996): taught question generation gave a median 0.36 on standardized tests and 0.86 on researcher-made tests. The pupils were *taught* with prompts such as question stems.
  - Ebersbach et al. (2020, university): generating questions matched testing and beat restudy at one week.
- **Drawing.**
  - Schmeck et al. (2014, Grade 8, a causal text on flu): comprehension d 0.85 and 0.52, with gains tracking how accurate the drawings were.
  - Van Meter et al. (2006): in Grades 4 and 6, supported drawing helped problem solving in Grade 6 only, with "no significant condition effects for fourth grade" and no effect on a multiple-choice test.
  - Fiorella & Mayer's median for drawing, d 0.40, is second-hand (Lawson & Mayer 2024) and mostly older learners.
  - So drawing per paragraph of History or Religion prose is not supported at 10. A partial diagram to complete, of a process in Science or Geography (the water cycle, digestion), is the defensible version.

### Read-aloud, text-to-speech, redundancy

- **Text-to-speech (Wood et al. 2018).** d 0.35 [0.14, 0.56] in students with reading difficulties.
- **Read-aloud as a test accommodation (Buzick & Stone 2014).** It helps everyone and more so pupils with disabilities. It is a testing aid, not a learning aid.
- **Accommodations for ADHD (Lovett & Nelson 2021, JAACAP, systematic review).** "most accommodations fail to show evidence of benefits that are specific to students with ADHD … An exception is read-aloud accommodations, which have two randomized experiments finding specific benefits for younger students with ADHD". They also say accommodations should come "only … along with evidence-based interventions".
- **Redundancy (Adesope & Nesbit 2012, 57 studies, mostly post-secondary).** Spoken plus written text was equivalent to written alone and better than spoken alone. The advantage held for low prior knowledge, system-paced and picture-free material. Showing key terms beat a full verbatim transcript.
- **Finger tracking.** I found no study, so it is not rated.

### Guidelines

- **NICE NG87 (2018, updated 2019).**
  - Environmental modifications come first: medication for children 5+ only if impairment remains "after environmental modifications have been implemented and reviewed" (1.5.13).
  - The glossary lists "optimising work or education to have shorter periods of focus with movement breaks (including the use of 'I need a break' cards)", fewer distractions, and written instructions.
  - It also recommends group parent training with "liaison with school".
- **AAP 2019 (Wolraich et al.), key action statement 5b, ages 6–11.** Medication "along with PTBM and/or behavioral classroom intervention (preferably both)". Educational interventions and instructional supports are "a necessary part of any treatment plan" (Grade A). It warns that accommodations without skill-building may "lead to reduced expectations".
- **CADDRA 4.1 (2020), Table 4.3, expert consensus with no grades.** "Provide immediate and frequent feedback", "Chunk and break down steps", "Reduce the amount of work required to show knowledge", "Increase change and introduce novelty", and at home "Allow planned frequent movement breaks".
- **What the guidelines say about the mechanics.** None endorses a specific study technique. None mentions timers as pressure, lockouts or lives.

## What fits this app

The core is a new exercise kind for Ιστορία, Γεωγραφία, Φυσικά and Θρησκευτικά. It is authored at development time from the mirrored Ε΄ books, the way `tools/problem-gen/language/` writes the language items: each item names its lesson and page, and an audit proves each key from the text. **Build on these (the evidence supports them):**

- **Read a section, then answer.** Show one section of the lesson (Gemini's "one paragraph at a time"; NICE/CADDRA chunking). Hide the text while she answers, so it is retrieval rather than look-up. Then ask 2–3 cued questions on it, using the renderers that already exist:
  - multiple choice
  - fill-blank with word chips (the cloze idea)
  - match-pairs (term → meaning)
  - ordering (History's timelines)
  - End each lesson with one whole-lesson ordering or matching item. Recall over the whole text beat per-section recall in ADHD students (Stern & Halamish 2023), and her recall is the less organised one.
  - Every fact that matters needs its own question. Testing does not transfer to untested facts.
- **The forgiveness ladder as it is.**
  - On the forgiving rung, the hint after the first wrong try, then «Δείξε μου», is exactly "feedback after every try". That is the single biggest moderator (g 0.73 vs 0.39), and it removes multiple-choice lure effects.
  - A plain exercise losing its star at the first wrong try is a mild, one-off cost on a reward that never goes below zero. That is the defensible kind of response cost.
  - Keep easy multiple choice as the entry rung. Recall-first ("type it" or "pick from chips without options") can be a harder rung for when she gets most first tries right. ADHD retrieval benefit disappeared when first tries mostly failed.
- **"Prove it" as feedback, not a gate.** After an answer, or with «Δείξε μου», show the section again with the supporting sentence lit. Author each question with the id of its sentence, so the audit can check that the key is in it. Asking her to find the sentence *before* answering turns retrieval into look-up.
- **Retries (#136), "shown, then do".** These are already spaced (the next days), rephrased (another item of the same topic) and limited to one a day.
  - Natural extension, if wanted: also owe back an item answered wrong at first, not only one shown worked.
  - The `retryFor` rule (same family, else same topic) maps to "same fact, other wording". For prose items, "family" would be the fact or question group.
- **The paint step for "find the main idea".** The `marked` rung (tap the marked phrases) is author-chosen highlighting, the version that helps school pupils (0.48). `paint` (freehand, checked against authored targets, with strays framed) is the version with feedback.
  - Use 🟢 for "main idea" and skip "surprising facts".
  - Gate the harder rung by the same `ConfigUser.problemReading` setting.
- **The owl's voice for read-aloud.** `tools/help-voice/` already records Greek clips at development time and audits them with Whisper. The same pipeline can record each section.
  - Make it a ▶/⏸ she controls, with the text on screen, which matches self-paced and written + spoken. Light up key terms rather than word-by-word karaoke.
  - Size: `public/` media is precached on every kiosk (vite.config.ts `globPatterns`), so a year of section audio may need to live outside the precache or be compressed. Decide that before recording.
- **Stars per section.** Stars per section can be small and frequent: rich early, thinned later as she settles (De Meyer 2019), and only ever added (`awardStars` only adds; the stars invariant). Pair them with words about what she did ("3 από 3 αιτίες"), which raises interest where tangible rewards can lower it.
- **Choice.** Offer two at a time ("Ιστορία ή Γεωγραφία πρώτα;", or which of two sections), with no extra reward for the choice itself (Patall 2008).
- **Routines for the session frame.**
  - A flow can open the study block with a short movement task (acute exercise helps inhibition), then a study task with its minutes shown.
  - The routine's time display is the calm kind of timer.
  - Late stars on a study task are a mild cost. If they turn the timer into pressure, set `lateStars` equal to the stars.
- **The parent at /parent.**
  - **Teach-back.** After a lesson, the parent's phone gets a card: «Ζήτα της να σου εξηγήσει…» with 2–3 authored "why/how" prompts and their answers. The parent asks and pushes back, which is the interactive part that makes teaching work.
  - **Stump the parent.** On the kiosk she builds a question from authored stems («Γιατί…;», «Τι έγινε μετά από…;») and word chips from the section, and the parent answers. The app can't judge free text with no runtime AI, and it needn't: the parent's answer is the check.
  - Ιστορικό already shows tries and what was shown per item.

**Leave out (conflicts with decisions already made, and the evidence agrees):**

- **Exponential cooldowns.** They conflict with the forgiving ladder: unlimited tries, help that grows with each wrong try, and «the player never sticks» (#136). The ladder gives what a cooldown aims at, an end to blind guessing, without the penalty.
- **Combo multiplier that resets on a miss, and hearts/lives.** They conflict with stars that only add, with `problemStars` paying at least 1 on forgiving, and with the home screen's practice of refusing in words, never punishing.
- **A 60 s timed boss fight.** It conflicts with how the app treats time: no exercise has a clock, and the one safety timer stops while covered (#51/#72). Keep the end-of-block review. Drop the clock and the hearts.
- **Fidget toys and gum.** Offline only. Don't recommend them.
- **"Surprising facts" and free sketch-noting.** No support at this age.

**Constraints for whoever builds it:**

- Every word the kids' screens say passes `check-gender` (no «έτοιμη», «ποια»). The parent prompts above are addressed to the parent, on /parent, so they are outside the kids' text.
- All content, rephrasings, marks and audio are written and audited at development time from `materials/`. Nothing reads `materials/` at runtime, and the Pi needs no internet.
- Θρησκευτικά has only the student book and no interactive chapters in the index. Its items would cite pages of 10-0242 only.
